11  The Middlegame: From Doctrine to a Real Package

11.1 The gap this document exists to close

Be honest about what the folder contained before this document. The conceptual chapters justify a worldview. The doctrine compresses it into defaults. The field guide shows positions — snapshots of finished packages with the pattern each one exhibits. The playbooks give opening theory — the first eight moves for six recognizable situations. In chess terms, the collection had openings, annotated positions, and endgame tables, and was missing the genre that actually teaches judgment: annotated full games, where you watch the decisions get made in sequence, interacting, under pressure, with the alternatives visible and the concessions priced.

The gap is structural, not editorial. Doctrine is stated per decision — one principle, one rung, one encoding choice at a time — but a real package is a system of coupled decisions: the presence policy constrains the round-trip law, which constrains the open/closed choice, which feeds back into what the validators may assume. Toy examples cannot exhibit coupling, because coupling only appears when there are enough decisions to couple. This is why the mature packages — ellmer, ragnar, quickr, rtemis — feel categorically more complicated than anything in the playbooks: they are not applying more patterns; they are absorbing the interactions.

So this document does two things. First it re-reads the mature four, not for their patterns (the field guide’s job) but for their pressure chains — the sequence by which one hard requirement forced a cascade of design consequences, including the concessions. Then it plays one annotated game at realistic fidelity: a style-document core, derived decision by decision, with the doctrine cited where it was followed and overruled where reality outvoted it. The code for the load-bearing spine was verified against S7 0.2.2 on R 4.6.1.

11.2 Part I: How the mature four absorbed complexity

11.2.1 ellmer: one requirement forces the hybrid

The instructive way to read ellmer is to ask what forced each piece of its architecture, and the answer starts with a product requirement, not a philosophy: streaming. A streamed response is a value that does not exist yet — tokens accumulate over seconds, consumers want them as they arrive, and the eventual Turn is only a fact once the stream closes. Partial state must live somewhere, and no amount of value-semantics conviction changes that. That single requirement makes the mutable Chat object load-bearing: R6 is not a stylistic lapse but the honest home for an accumulation-in-progress, and the doctrine’s actual contribution is containment — completed turns crystallize into immutable S7 values, the conversation is reconstructible from the values alone, and the mutable rim stays one class wide.

The second chain: provider variation. Every supported provider has quirks — capabilities, wire formats, authentication, streaming protocols — and the Provider dispatch surface absorbs them. The price, visible if you watch the package evolve, is that a plugin surface grows: each new provider capability becomes a new generic or a new argument somewhere, and the interface can never again be reorganized cheaply because third parties stand on it. The lesson for the middlegame: doctrine 10 tells you to design the verb vocabulary carefully; ellmer shows why the stakes are asymmetric — under-designing an internal function costs a refactor, under-designing an extension surface costs an ecosystem migration.

11.2.2 ragnar: the price tag on base-type inheritance

The compendium praises MarkdownDocumentChunks inheriting from tibble as “honest base-type inheritance” — the value really is a table. The middlegame reading adds the invoice: inheriting a base type means the parent’s entire API becomes your unvalidated mutator set. Every dplyr verb, every [ method, every tool in the tibble ecosystem can transform the object along paths where the subclass’s laws are never consulted — drop the columns the class depends on, break the alignment with @document, or silently strip the subclass entirely. ragnar’s concession is that its invariants on that surface are documented rather than enforced, and re-established at the operations that matter. That is a rational trade — the whole point of inheriting tibble is to get that ecosystem — but it is a trade, and the doctrine’s “construction is the proof” holds only on the package’s own front doors, not on the thousand side doors the parent class ships with. When you inherit a base type, you are choosing which of those doors to guard, and accepting the rest.

11.2.3 quickr: power through refusal

quickr compiles R to Fortran, and the design decision that makes it possible is what it declines: it accepts a subset of R, requires declare(type()) annotations, and errors on constructs it cannot compile faithfully rather than approximating their semantics. This is doctrine 17 — refuse to guess — applied not to an argument default but to the entire domain of the tool: shrink the promise until you can keep it. The middlegame lesson generalizes to every “model an existing thing” project: the fastest route to a robust core is usually not handling more of the input space, it is drawing a smaller boundary you can defend completely, with loud errors at the fence. Coverage grows later, fence post by fence post, each one tested. The packages that tried to accept everything on day one are the ones whose validators are full of guesses.

11.2.4 rtemis: the fixed cost of constraints-as-data

rtemis’s property-factory stratum — the PropertySpec machinery deriving validators, defaults, JSON Schema, and TUI forms from single declarations — is the compendium’s favorite exhibit for constraints-as-data. The middlegame reading prices it: that stratum is itself a serious system, hundreds of lines of meta-machinery that every contributor must understand before they can add a property, and every error message now has an extra layer of indirection between the symptom and the declaration. It amortizes because rtemis has hundreds of hyperparameters across dozens of models and genuinely needs all four projections. Below some scale threshold — and most packages are below it — hand-written new_property() calls are not a failure to apply the pattern; they are the pattern correctly not applied (doctrine 15: the third occurrence, not the first). The factory stratum is the right move exactly when you can name the projections you need and count the declarations that will pay for it.

11.2.5 The shared shape

Four packages, one shape: each met a pressure that made the naive, doctrine-by-the-letter design uneconomic — streaming broke pure values, ecosystem reuse broke enforced invariants, R’s full semantics broke compilability, hyperparameter count broke hand-written validation. And each responded the same way: not by abandoning a principle but by moving where it applies — containing state instead of forbidding it, guarding chosen doors instead of all doors, shrinking the promise instead of diluting it, buying machinery only where the census showed volume. That move — relocating a principle’s jurisdiction instead of revoking it — is the core middlegame skill, and it is exactly what per-decision doctrine cannot teach and an annotated game can.

11.3 Part II: An annotated game

The domain: a core for map-style documents — the root, its sources, its layers, their paint. Chosen because it is real, the spec pressure is genuine (hundreds of paint properties, embedded expressions, ongoing spec evolution), and it exercises three playbooks at once (spec modeling, embedded language, and the temptation of a stateful manager). What follows is a derivation, not project work: ten decisions in dependency order, each with its pressure, its options, and its resolution — doctrine cited when followed, named when overruled.

11.3.1 Move 1: Scope — model what your operations touch

The census (playbook 1, move 2) says the spec is far too large to model at once: root, sources, layers, sprite, glyphs, terrain, fog, transitions, and a paint vocabulary in the hundreds. The pressure is real and the answer is an ordering, not an ambition: model the nodes your operations touch — construction, mutation, serialization of root/sources/layers — and let everything else pass through as inert wire data until an operation needs it. Deferral is not debt if the pass-through is lossless; it is the vertical-slice discipline applied to scope.

11.3.2 Move 2: Rung 0 — the engine’s validator, and gold files

The engine ships an authoritative validator; re-implementing it in R would be expression-system envy at document scale. Rung 0 is: gold files produced by reference tooling, round-trip tests against them, and the engine validator run in CI where a JS runtime is available. Every subsequent decision about what we validate assumes this floor exists.

11.3.3 Move 3: The layer family — three principles collide

The pivotal decision of the whole game. Layers come in a dozen types (fill, line, symbol, raster, …) that differ in behavior and in their paint vocabularies — doctrine 8 says subclass family, and dispatch genuinely wants it (validation, legend generation, rendering hints all vary by type). But each type’s paint vocabulary is dozens of properties, churned by spec releases — hand-modeling four hundred paint properties as S7 properties is schema cosplay by volume, and doctrine 16 says generate tables, not class graphs. Meanwhile the paint values themselves can be literals or expressions, which doctrine 8 would encode as a union.

The resolution is a two-level hybrid, with the split placed exactly at the dispatch boundary: layer types become subclasses, because behavior dispatches on them; paint becomes a validated bag — one list property, checked against a generated spec table. The bag is a denormalization doctrine 8 would frown at, chosen consciously: variant honesty is paid for where dispatch collects the benefit (the type level) and declined where it would cost four hundred properties tracking a moving spec (the paint level). The spec table is constraints-as-data — rtemis’s pattern at the scale where it actually amortizes:

# generated from the engine's style-spec json, one row per paint property;
# excerpt shown - the real table is machine-derived, never hand-edited
paint_spec <- list(
  fill = list(
    `fill-color`   = list(type = "color"),
    `fill-opacity` = list(type = "number", minimum = 0, maximum = 1)
  ),
  line = list(
    `line-color` = list(type = "color"),
    `line-width` = list(type = "number", minimum = 0)
  )
)

check_paint <- function(paint, type) {
  spec <- paint_spec[[type]]
  bad <- character()
  for (nm in names(paint)) {
    rule <- spec[[nm]]
    if (is.null(rule)) {
      bad <- c(bad, paste0("unknown paint property '", nm, "'"))
      next
    }
    val <- paint[[nm]]
    if (is_expression(val)) next   # move 4: rung 1, the engine judges these
    if (identical(rule$type, "number") && is.numeric(val)) {
      if (!is.null(rule$minimum) && val < rule$minimum) {
        bad <- c(bad, paste0("'", nm, "' below minimum ", rule$minimum))
      }
      if (!is.null(rule$maximum) && val > rule$maximum) {
        bad <- c(bad, paste0("'", nm, "' above maximum ", rule$maximum))
      }
    }
  }
  if (length(bad)) bad
}

Note what the interaction did: doctrine 16 (generate tables) is what made overruling doctrine 8 (honest variants) safe — the bag is lawless only as a class shape; the table restores the laws at validation time, and the same table can later project documentation or a paint-builder UI (the reverse arrow of 06-documents-schemas-dsls.qmd). Neither principle alone produces this design; their collision does.

11.3.4 Move 4: Expressions — rung 1, stated as a policy not an omission

Paint values may be expressions (["interpolate", ["zoom"], ...]). The escalation ladder says opaque passthrough until analysis is the product, so check_paint() contains one line — if (is_expression(val)) next — and that line is the policy: expressions are the engine’s jurisdiction. The trap this dodges is the validator slowly learning the expression grammar, one special case at a time, until it is a bad reimplementation of the engine (expression-system envy). The upgrade path is already written down: if legend generation ever becomes a requirement, that is the fields_used() justification test from 09-playbooks.qmd, and the climb goes to combinators, not directly to an AST.

11.3.5 Move 5: The layer classes — thin, because the table is thick

layer <- S7::new_class("layer", abstract = TRUE, properties = list(
  id     = S7::class_character,
  source = S7::class_character,
  paint  = S7::new_property(S7::class_list, default = list())
))

fill_layer <- S7::new_class("fill_layer", parent = layer,
  validator = function(self) check_paint(self@paint, "fill")
)
line_layer <- S7::new_class("line_layer", parent = layer,
  validator = function(self) check_paint(self@paint, "line")
)

Each subclass validator is one delegation line. This is the checking ladder holding under load: type lives on the property classes, paint law lives in the table, and the subclass’s only job is to bind its type tag to the table lookup. A dozen layer types cost a dozen three-line classes — which is also the honest test that the move-3 hybrid was right: had we hand-modeled paint, each of these classes would be a hundred lines of property declarations chasing the spec.

11.3.6 Move 6: Presence and the round-trip law — decided together, or wrong

Paint properties have engine-defined defaults (fill-opacity defaults to 1 in the engine). The presence decision: do not store engine defaults. An omitted key means “engine default,” and the model preserves the distinction between omitted and explicitly set to the default value — because a user who wrote fill-opacity: 1 and a user who wrote nothing have expressed different things, and a future spec release could change the default out from under the silent one. This immediately constrains the round-trip law (the equality taxonomy of 06-documents-schemas-dsls.qmd): the promise is canonical equality on modeled fields and representational preservation on the paint bag — not wire equivalence, precisely because wire equivalence would license resolving defaults, which is the one normalization this design forbids. Two decisions, one lock: neither the presence policy nor the equality relation could have been chosen alone.

11.3.7 Move 7: The root, and the laws only it can see

style <- S7::new_class("style", properties = list(
  sources = S7::new_property(S7::class_list, default = list()),
  layers  = S7::new_property(S7::class_list, default = list())
), validator = function(self) {
  ids  <- vapply(self@layers, function(l) l@id, character(1))
  refs <- vapply(self@layers, function(l) l@source, character(1))
  msgs <- character()
  if (anyDuplicated(ids)) msgs <- c(msgs, "layer ids must be unique")
  missing_refs <- setdiff(refs, names(self@sources))
  if (length(missing_refs)) {
    msgs <- c(msgs, paste("layer references unknown source:", missing_refs))
  }
  if (length(msgs)) msgs
})

Id uniqueness and source resolution are laws no layer can check — they mention the collection. The aggregate-root rung earns its place here, and note the granularity call (an open question the ledger tracks): this design has one root despite the document’s depth, because both cross-object laws happen to be visible from the top. A spec with cross-references three levels down would force the nested-root question this game gets to skip.

11.3.8 Move 8: Mutators — the front door, with preconditions

add_layer <- function(style, layer, before = NULL) {
  layers <- style@layers
  if (!is.null(before)) {
    ids <- vapply(layers, function(l) l@id, character(1))
    pos <- match(before, ids)
    if (is.na(pos)) {
      cli::cli_abort("no layer with id {.val {before}} to insert before.")
    }
    layers <- append(layers, list(layer), after = pos - 1)
  } else {
    layers <- c(layers, list(layer))
  }
  S7::set_props(style, layers = layers)
}

before not existing is an operation error (rung: method precondition), distinct from the structural laws below it — the message can therefore speak the operation’s language (“insert before”), which no class validator could. And a verified interaction worth knowing: set_props() re-runs the root validator, so a duplicate id introduced through the mutator is rejected by the root’s law without the mutator checking it — the rungs genuinely do not duplicate each other, and the insertion order, precondition failure, and duplicate rejection were all confirmed live.

11.3.9 Move 9: Ids — the tier-1 answer to the atoms-versus-earning tension

Should layer_id be a semantic type? It is a meaning, not just a string — doctrine 1 wants it named early; doctrine 15 wants abstraction earned. The concrete resolution uses the tiers: a reused property definition (tier 1 — one new_property() value shared by every id-shaped slot) names the meaning and centralizes its law at near-zero cost, while a full class (tier 2/3) waits until something dispatches on ids or ids grow structure. The tension between two principles, unresolvable in the abstract, costs one line to resolve in the concrete — which is itself a middlegame lesson: many doctrinal collisions are cheap at the point of decision and only look profound in prose.

11.3.10 Move 10: The manager that must not exist

Every document model eventually tempts its author with a StyleManager R6 class — “convenient” mutable state holding the current style, an undo stack, a dirty flag. The fact/process sort (playbook 5) says no: a style is a fact through and through; reading, patching, and writing it are shell functions; and an undo stack, if wanted, is a list of immutable snapshots — which value semantics makes nearly free (structural sharing: the copies share every untouched payload, per the verified probes in 02-values-and-state.qmd). The mutable manager would buy nothing and sell local reasoning. Declined, by name, on the record.

11.3.11 The game’s concessions, tallied

An annotated game ends by admitting what it conceded, because the concessions are where the doctrine’s edges live:

  • The paint bag is a flattened product (overrules the letter of doctrine 8), made safe by the generated table (doctrine 16) — the overrule and its mitigation are one decision, not two.
  • Unknown paint keys are currently rejected (closed node), which is the strict choice; a package prioritizing round-trip fidelity for foreign styles would flip this to a pass-through with the table checking only known keys. The choice is defensible either way; making it by accident is not. This one is flagged for revisit.
  • The spec table is trusted, not verified — its correctness is inherited from the generation pipeline against the engine’s spec JSON, which quietly relocates rung 0 upstream. A stale table is now the design’s single most dangerous failure mode, which is exactly the single-source-of-truth trade: one datum, one point of failure.
  • What the toy still hides: the real table generation pipeline, the sprite and glyph subsystems, wire-version migration, and every print method, error message, and documentation page — the unglamorous majority of a real package that no design document shortens much.

11.4 Part III: The loop that actually transfers the wisdom

The last honest thing this document can say is that no document — not this one — converts wisdom into skill. The mature four were not designed by consulting a compendium; they were designed by people making decisions, hitting walls, and revising. What a compendium can do is shorten that loop: make the decision points visible sooner, the options nameable, and the concessions explicit instead of accidental. The transfer mechanism is a practice, and it is cheap:

Keep a design journal in the project. One entry per real decision, five lines: the decision, the pressure, the options considered, the choice, and the principle followed or overruled, with the reason. The annotated game above is exactly this journal, written after the fact; writing it during the fact is faster and truer. The journal does two jobs at once: it makes “selectively considering the proper aspects” an explicit act instead of an anxiety (you consulted the doctrine, you cited or overruled it, done — no lingering doubt about whether you should have used some sophisticated pattern you decided against), and it produces the evidence stream the compendium needs to stay alive — a recurring overrule in your journals is a counterexample entry for open-questions.qmd, and a pattern that keeps earning across your projects is a promotion candidate.

Review the journal at complexity checkpoints, not on a schedule. The natural checkpoints: the first mutator (is the invariant placement holding?), the first serializer (did presence and equality get decided together?), the tenth class (is a factory stratum starting to amortize?), the first external implementor (is the interface really a contract?). These are the moments the middlegame pressure chains start, and a half-hour against the doctrine’s decision guides at each one is worth more than any amount of up-front design piety.

That is the whole method: openings from the playbooks, this document’s annotated game as the model for the middlegame, the journal as your own annotation habit, and the ledger as the place your games feed back into the theory. Eating our own food is not a property the compendium can have by itself — it is a loop the compendium and the projects run together.
